Motorcycle Gear Guide

Why Gear Matters

Protective riding gear is the only barrier between a rider and the road surface in a crash. Unlike a car, a motorcycle offers no structural protection — the rider is fully exposed. High-quality gear significantly reduces the severity of road rash, fractures, and head injuries.

Helmets

A helmet is the single most important piece of motorcycle safety equipment. It protects the head and brain in a crash and can mean the difference between a minor injury and a fatal one.

There are four main helmet types:

Full-face helmets provide the most protection. They cover the entire head and face, including the chin — which studies show is one of the most commonly impacted areas in a crash. Full-face helmets also reduce wind noise and fatigue on long rides. They are the recommended choice for most riders, especially beginners.

Modular (flip-up) helmets have a hinged chin bar that can be lifted. This offers convenience — you can lift the chin to talk or take a drink without removing the helmet — while still providing full-face protection when closed. However, the hinge mechanism adds weight and can be a weak point in a crash compared to a solid full-face.

Open-face (three-quarter) helmets cover the top, sides, and back of the head but leave the face exposed. They offer more airflow and visibility and are popular for cruiser riding and urban commuting. However, they provide no chin or face protection.

Half helmets (also called skull caps or brain buckets) cover only the top of the head. They offer minimal protection compared to other types and are not recommended for high-speed riding.

When selecting a helmet, look for certification markings: DOT (Department of Transportation) in the US, ECE 22.06 in Europe, or SNELL certification for highest impact performance. Always buy a new helmet — a used or previously crashed helmet may have invisible internal damage that compromises protection.

Helmet fit is critical. A helmet should fit snugly all around with no pressure points. It should not rock forward or backward when you shake your head. The cheek pads should press firmly against your cheeks. A helmet that is too loose can come off in a crash; one that is too tight causes pain on longer rides.

Helmets should be replaced every five years regardless of appearance, as the foam liner degrades over time and loses its ability to absorb impact energy.

Jackets

A motorcycle jacket protects the torso, arms, and elbows. Good jackets include CE-rated armor at the shoulders and elbows and have a back protector pocket or built-in back protector.

Leather jackets offer excellent abrasion resistance and have been the traditional choice for decades. Full-grain cowhide leather is the most durable. Leather jackets are heavy and can be hot in warm weather.

Textile (synthetic) jackets are made from materials like Cordura nylon or ballistic polyester. They are lighter and more versatile than leather, often featuring waterproof liners and ventilation zippers. High-quality textile jackets with CE Level 2 armor can offer protection comparable to leather.

Look for jackets with CE Level 1 or Level 2 certified armor at the elbows, shoulders, and back (Level 2 is higher protection). An armor certification label inside the pocket indicates the level.

Gloves

Hands are almost always the first thing to hit the ground in a fall — the instinct to put your hands out to break a fall is nearly universal. Motorcycle gloves protect against road rash, abrasion, and impact.

Short (gauntlet-free) gloves are suitable for warm weather and urban riding. Long gauntlet gloves extend over the wrist and provide better protection against wind chill and wrist injury in a fall.

Look for gloves with palm sliders (a reinforced pad on the palm), knuckle protectors, and wrist closure. Leather is the most abrasion-resistant material. Textile gloves are better for wet conditions and temperature regulation.

Never ride without gloves. Even a low-speed fall on a rough road surface can cause severe road rash to bare hands within a fraction of a second of contact.

Boots

Motorcycle boots protect the feet, ankles, and lower legs from impact, crushing, and road contact. In a crash, the ankle is one of the most commonly injured joints.

Purpose-built motorcycle boots have rigid ankle protection, oil-resistant non-slip soles, and reinforced toe boxes. Casual shoes and sneakers offer almost no protection against twisting or crushing forces.

Boots should cover the ankle fully. Look for boots with CE-certified ankle armor or reinforced ankle cups. The sole should be grippy enough for walking but stiff enough to provide support on the footpeg.

Tall (mid-calf or knee-height) boots offer more protection than short boots but can be inconvenient for walking. Short motorcycle-specific boots that look like regular footwear are available and still provide much more protection than regular shoes.

Riding Pants and Full-Body Considerations

Riding pants protect the hips, thighs, and knees. Leather or textile motorcycle pants with CE-rated knee and hip armor are significantly better than jeans, even reinforced riding jeans, in a high-speed crash.

For maximum protection, consider a matched suit (jacket and pants that zip together) or a one-piece race suit, which eliminates the gap at the waist that can expose skin in a slide.

Hi-Visibility Gear

High-visibility (hi-vis) gear — typically bright yellow, orange, or lime green with reflective strips — increases a rider's conspicuity to other road users. Studies show that hi-vis gear reduces the likelihood of other drivers failing to see a motorcyclist. Reflective panels on the back of a jacket or helmet are especially valuable at night.
