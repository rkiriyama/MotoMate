Motorcycle Safety and Riding Techniques

Defensive Riding

Defensive riding is a mindset as much as a skill. It means anticipating potential hazards before they become emergencies and positioning yourself on the road to maximize your visibility and escape options.

Assume that other drivers may not see you. Motorcycles are smaller than cars and can disappear into blind spots easily. Avoid lingering in a vehicle's blind spot — either accelerate past or drop back to where you are clearly visible. Make eye contact with drivers at intersections when possible before proceeding.

Maintain a following distance of at least three seconds behind the vehicle ahead. This gives you time to react to sudden stops. In poor weather or low-visibility conditions, increase this to four or five seconds.

Choose your lane position strategically. The left third of a lane (position one) is often preferred because it increases your visibility to oncoming drivers and keeps you away from road debris near the edge. Avoid riding in the oil strip that runs down the center of a lane, especially at traffic lights where cars drip fluids.

Road Hazards

Common road hazards for motorcyclists include gravel, sand, wet leaves, oil patches, painted road markings (which become slippery when wet), rail tracks, and road debris. These surfaces dramatically reduce tire grip.

When encountering a hazard you cannot avoid, try to be upright and avoid braking or steering aggressively while on the surface. If you must brake on a slippery surface, apply brakes gently and progressively before reaching the hazard when possible.

Potholes and road irregularities should be spotted early. Scan the road surface 12 to 15 seconds ahead. If a pothole cannot be avoided, stand slightly on the footpegs to absorb the impact with your legs rather than transmitting it entirely through the frame.

Be especially cautious at intersections — studies consistently show that intersections are the most dangerous location for motorcycle crashes. Slow down when approaching intersections, even when you have the right of way, and watch for vehicles that may run red lights or turn across your path.

Cornering Technique

Cornering is one of the most important skills a motorcyclist can develop. The basic principle is slow-in, fast-out: reduce speed before the corner, not during it.

Approach a corner from the outside of your lane to maximize your view through the corner and give yourself the widest possible arc. Look through the corner toward where you want to go — your motorcycle will naturally follow your eyes. As you identify the apex (the tightest point of the turn), begin to roll on the throttle smoothly. Steady or increasing throttle keeps the suspension settled and the bike stable.

Avoid braking mid-corner if possible, as this compresses the front fork and reduces ground clearance. If you enter a corner too fast, resist the urge to brake hard; instead, look further through the turn, push the handlebar to lean the bike more, and roll on the throttle gently to stabilize.

Counter-steering is the technique used to steer a motorcycle at speeds above approximately 15 mph. To turn right, you push forward on the right handlebar, which causes the bike to lean right and turn right. This is the primary steering input at all normal road speeds and is essential to master for confident cornering.

Emergency Braking

Emergency braking requires both brakes applied together, progressively and firmly, without locking either wheel. Modern motorcycles with ABS (Anti-lock Braking System) will prevent wheel lock automatically, allowing the rider to apply maximum braking force without skidding.

On a motorcycle without ABS, squeeze the front brake lever firmly but progressively — a sudden grab can cause the front wheel to lock and the bike to go down. Apply rear brake simultaneously with moderate pressure. If the rear wheel skids, maintain the pressure — releasing a locked rear suddenly can cause a high-side crash. Keep the bike upright and steer it in a straight line while braking.

Practice emergency stops in a safe, empty area so the technique becomes instinctive. Many riders find they under-brake in emergencies because they have not trained the reflex to apply full braking force quickly.

Swerving and Hazard Avoidance

When an obstacle appears suddenly, swerving (changing direction quickly) is sometimes more effective than braking. A motorcycle can change direction faster than it can stop.

To swerve, push firmly on the handlebar in the direction you want to go (counter-steer), then immediately push the opposite bar to straighten the bike after clearing the hazard. Do not brake hard while initiating the swerve, as this destabilizes the bike.

For obstacles in your path, decide early whether to swerve left or right, factoring in traffic and road edge hazards.

Skid Recovery

A front wheel skid — caused by excessive front brake on a slippery or unstable surface — requires immediately releasing the front brake lever to allow the wheel to regain traction, then reapplying more gently.

A rear wheel skid — caused by excessive rear brake or throttle — requires keeping the rear brake applied if you are going straight (releasing it suddenly causes a high-side), or releasing gently while steering to keep the bike tracking straight. Avoid adding throttle during a skid.

Visibility and Positioning

Wear brightly colored or high-visibility gear. Most motorcycle crashes involving other vehicles occur because the other driver did not see the rider. Hi-vis yellow, orange, and reflective materials significantly increase conspicuity, especially in low-light conditions.

Use your headlight at all times, even during the day. Many modern motorcycles run with the headlight on automatically. Position yourself where you are most visible — generally not directly behind a vehicle, but offset slightly so drivers can see you in their mirrors.
